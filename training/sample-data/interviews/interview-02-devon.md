# Interview 02 — Devon, Operations Director (urgent-care clinics, buyer)

> Fictional transcript for training. Devon is the operations director for a chain of 8 urgent-care clinics, ~140 staff. He's the **buyer** — he chose ShiftLoop and rolled it out to his clinic managers. He is more technical than the average user and evaluates tools on labor cost and compliance. His view contradicts the managers' view in several places — that tension is the point.

**Interviewer:** You picked ShiftLoop for the whole chain. What were you solving for?

**Devon:** Labor cost and compliance, full stop. We were over-scheduling — managers padding shifts "just in case" — and we'd had two close calls with break-compliance rules that could've cost us real money. I wanted a system that enforces the rules and shows me labor spend against budget. The scheduling convenience for managers is nice, but that's not why I bought it.

**Interviewer:** How did the rollout go from your seat?

**Devon:** For me? Smooth. I set up the org, loaded locations, set the labor rules in an afternoon. I'll caveat that — I'm comfortable with this stuff, I've configured worse systems. So when my managers told me setup was a nightmare I was a little surprised, because for *me* it wasn't.

**Interviewer:** Tell me about that gap.

**Devon:** A couple of my clinic managers basically refused to onboard. One told me she spent forty-five minutes trying to get her staff list in and gave up, went back to her old spreadsheet, and I didn't find out for two weeks because the schedule was still getting done — just not in the tool I'm paying for. So now I've got a compliance system that's blind to half my locations. That's my actual problem. The tool only helps me if *they* use it, and getting them over the hump is where it falls apart.

**Interviewer:** What do you think makes them stall?

**Devon:** Honestly I think it's the first hour. These are people who manage a clinic floor; they don't have a spare hour to "set up software." If it's not basically done for them out of the box they'll route around it. And here's the thing they won't say but I'll say it: a lot of them don't want the system to take over scheduling because the manual juggling is part of how they feel in control. The auto-scheduler threatens that.

**Interviewer:** Interesting. You mentioned staff — does the staff phone app factor in?

**Devon:** [laughs] Look, everyone wants to sell me on the app. Half my staff don't open their email. The idea that they're going to download an app, log in, and check their shifts — for a chunk of my workforce that is just not happening. They look at a printout taped to the break-room wall. So when I hear "staff love the mobile experience," I think, sure, *some* do, the younger ones maybe. I would not bet a dollar of my budget on the app moving the needle. I'd bet it on getting my managers to actually publish in the system.

**Interviewer:** If activation — managers getting to a first published schedule — is the goal, what would move it most?

**Devon:** Take the setup away from them. Let me push the staff roster down to every location from the org level, since I already have all of it in our HR system, so the manager opens it and their people are *already there*. Don't make a clinic manager do data entry. And honestly, give them a "we made your first schedule, just review and publish" path so the blank page isn't sitting there daring them to quit. The auto-scheduler I was lukewarm on for cost reasons is maybe the thing that gets a reluctant manager to "done."

**Interviewer:** That's almost the opposite of what one of your managers might say about the auto-scheduler.

**Devon:** Oh, completely. They don't trust it, I want them to lean on it. We're not the same customer. You're going to have to thread that needle.
